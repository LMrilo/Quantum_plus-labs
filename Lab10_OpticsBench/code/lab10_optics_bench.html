1 · Kit anatomy

What will be on the table?

The lab asks one question in four steps: does the light on the bench come as single photons, and can one photon interfere with itself? You answer it from detector clicks alone, on the Thorlabs EDU-QOP1 Quantum Optics Kit. These are its parts:

Pump laser (405 nm, violet)
Drives the crystal in Parts B–D. Only the staff switch it on.
Crystal (the pair source)
Pumped at 405 nm, it emits two photons of about 810 nm at once. One photon of each pair goes to detector T, the other to the beam splitter.
Detectors T, A, B
Single-photon detectors. T is the herald arm; A and B sit at the two outputs of the beam splitter.
Beam splitter
A half-transparent mirror with two outputs (course document QC10, Light).
Michelson interferometer (Part D)
Placed by the staff in one beam-splitter output, with B behind it: a second beam splitter sends the light into two arms, mirrors send it back, and the arms recombine. A stage moves one mirror by $x$.
Time tagger and software
Records when each detector clicks and counts the coincidences; the software prints the rates and $g^{(2)}$ (defined below).
Alignment laser (635 nm, red)
In Part A, dimmed by the kit's filter, it replaces the crystal: A and B only, no T.

Words you need

Click
One pulse of a single-photon detector.
Coincidence and window $\Delta t$
Two (or three) detectors click in the same window $\Delta t$ (5 ns here, set in the software).
$g^{(2)}$
With $P(X)$ the probability that detector $X$ clicks in one window, $$g^{(2)} = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(A)\,P(B)}:$$ $1$ means no correlation, $< 1$ fewer coincidences than chance, $> 1$ more. On the bench $P(X) = R_X\,\Delta t$, with $R_X$ the click rate (clicks per second).
Accidentals
The coincidences expected by pure chance if A and B click independently, rate $R_A R_B\,\Delta t$; so $g^{(2)}$ = coincidences / accidentals.
Poisson light
Photons arriving independently at random times, like raindrops. A laser, however dim, is Poisson light.
Heralding
A click at T announces that its partner photon is on the way to the beam splitter; A and B are counted only together with T.
Interference and visibility
A wave split in two and recombined adds up or cancels, depending on the path difference; visibility $\mathcal{V} = (R_{\max} - R_{\min})/(R_{\max} + R_{\min})$, 1 for perfect fringes.
Standard deviation $\sigma$
The typical scatter of repeated results; a count of $N$ clicks scatters by about $\sqrt{N}$.
2 · Safety

Is it safe?

SAFETY FIRST
  • The room is dark during measurements.
  • The 405 nm pump laser is class 3B: only the staff switch it on (key, interlock; manual Ch. 1, Ch. 14).
  • While it is on (Parts B–D) you follow the i-LAMP eyewear and conduct rules of the laser safety officer. The manual is explicit: “Always wear laser safety glasses when working with the pump laser!” (manual §7.6.4).
  • You use the 635 nm alignment laser and the software.
3 · Predictions

What do you expect?

Each Part of the bench guide opens with a question. Write your prediction before you open the answers; at the bench you measure and compare. Nothing you type here is sent or saved.

PartQuestion Number to predictYour prediction
AIs a very dim laser a single-photon source? $g^{(2)}$ of the dimmed laser (A and B)
BDoes the crystal really make pairs? coincidences T&A / accidentals
CCan one photon take both exits? $g^{(2)}$ of heralded photons (T, A, B)
DDoes a single photon interfere with itself? fringe period in mirror position; wavelength $\lambda$
Show the answers

A: $g^{(2)} \approx 1$. Poisson light stays Poisson however dim: each photon picks its exit on its own. The manual's example (§9.1, Table 1) gives 1.0020.

B: the manual expects at least 30 (§9.2); its example (§9.4, Table 2) gives ≈ 115: coincidences are about 115 times the accidentals.

C: $g^{(2)} < 1$: one photon cannot take both exits, and any classical light obeys $g^{(2)} \ge 1$. Table 2 gives 0.0190, about 1170 standard deviations below 1.

D: the light goes out and back, so moving the mirror by $x$ changes the path by $2x$: the fringe period is $\lambda/2 \approx 405$ nm in mirror position, and the spacing of the maxima gives back $\lambda \approx 810$ nm.

Photon statistics at a beam splitter

THE QUESTION Manual §9.1 estimates that only 0.004 photons are inside the dimmed-laser setup at any moment. Does such rare light behave like single photons? And does a heralded photon ever turn up at both exits?

The playbox runs the two sources window by window through an ideal 50:50 beam splitter and ideal detectors (no loss, no dark counts). Per window the number of photons (laser) or pairs (heralded) is Poisson with mean $\mu$ or $m$; each photon then picks its exit, A or B, like a coin toss. Every click counts toward $g^{(2)}$ exactly as on the bench.

Estimators.$g^{(2)}$ = coincidences seen / expected by chance. With $W$ windows, chance expects $N_A N_B/W$ coincidences (laser) or $N_{TA} N_{TB}/N_T$ triples (heralded; a triple is T&A and T&B both coincident), the bench guide's formulas. The error bar $\sigma \approx g^{(2)}/\sqrt{N}$, with $N$ the coincidences seen, is shown from $N = 10$ on. The distance from chance, (expected − seen)/√expected, is shown once chance expects at least 1.
Laser.Each photon picks its exit on its own, so A and B click independently: $g^{(2)} = 1$ exactly, at every $\mu$. Dimming does not change that; it only makes coincidences rarer, so after the same number of windows $N_{AB}$ is smaller and the error bar wider. Try $\mu = 0.1$, then $0.01$.
Heralded.One pair gives one click at T and one photon at A or B, never both. A triple needs two pairs in the same window, so the ideal value is $g^{(2)} = 1 - e^{-m} \approx m$ for small $m$: far below the classical bound 1, and the smaller $m$, the lower.

REAL-WORLD ANCHORS A: Hanbury Brown and Twiss (1956) correlated two detectors' signals, the method behind Part A; it measured the sizes of stars. C: Grangier, Roger and Aspect (1986) ran Part C with heralded photons from atoms and found 0.18; today Quandela builds quantum-dot single-photon sources for photonic quantum computers. D: LIGO's kilometre-long Michelson interferometers reported gravitational waves (2016).
