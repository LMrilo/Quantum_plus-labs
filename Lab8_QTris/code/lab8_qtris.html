Original game: QTris e Meccanica Quantistica — Alioscia Hamma / AH Quantum Lab, NQSTI online edition 2024. Adapted with full attribution; see the credits section below.

Prerequisites: Labs 5–7 1 ECTS ≈ 8 h lab work Mode: Playable Board Game + Qiskit

Rationale

THE DRIVING QUESTION Every cell of the board is full from move one, and yet nobody knows who's winning. What kind of board game can't tell you its own score until the dice say so?

QTris plays tic-tac-toe on a 3×3 grid whose nine cells are nine qubits. Every tile is a quantum state, every move card is a reversible operation, and the endgame is a dice-resolved measurement that collapses the whole board to black and white before a single point can be scored. Its rulebook makes a bold claim: there is no fundamental difference between QTris and quantum mechanics. That claim is checkable — and this lab checks it. Every probability in this digital adaptation is computed by an engine whose numbers were verified, state by state and table by table, against the game's printed measurement tables. Where the print and the physics agree (38 of 40 rows, within a stated rounding of at most half a percentage point), you play the print. Where they provably cannot both be right (two rows — see the erratum panel), the physics wins, openly.

You will play first and formalize second. The tally counters, the printed dice tables, and an optional exact mode are all part of the instrument: the game is not an analogy for quantum mechanics — it is a quantum system you operate by hand, the same physics that runs Stern-Gerlach spin measurements and commercial quantum random-number generators.

Learning Outcomes

  • Translate every QTris component into quantum-mechanical language: tiles as states $|W\rangle = |0\rangle$, $|B\rangle = |1\rangle$, $|R\rangle = |+\rangle$, $|L\rangle = |-\rangle$; cards as unitaries; dice as the Born rule.
  • Predict measurement statistics for single tiles and entangled pairs, decorated or not, and confirm them against both the printed d100 tables and the exact probabilities.
  • Explain why the pink-square gate is a single canonical rotation $R(-60°)$, and how one rotation produces the game's 25, 75, 93 and 7 — including where the $\sqrt{3}$ comes from.
  • Distinguish Procedure 1 and Procedure 2 for a rotated-basis measurement: identical statistics, different post-measurement states — on the game's own tiles.
  • Recognize state-dependent rotational invariance: why two pink squares cancel exactly on two of the four entangled pair types and provably not on the other two — and how that catches a printed table in an error its own authors missed.
  • Operate sequential incompatible measurements (color vs orientation) and connect them to Stern-Gerlach physics and spin qubits.

Part 1 — The Board: Nine Cells, Nine Qubits

QUESTION FIRST Every cell is full from move one, and yet nobody knows who's winning. What kind of board game can't tell you its own score until the dice say so?

A game of QTris has three phases. In preparation, all nine cells are filled with tiles. In the operations phase, the players alternate for ten turns (five each), each turn drawing two cards and playing two — cards act on any cell; there is no cell ownership. In the measurement phase, the cells are visited in order 1→9 and every unresolved tile is collapsed by a dice roll to plain white or black. Only then is the board scored: one point per three-in-a-row of your color over the eight lines, most points wins.

The tiles are states of a two-level system — a qubit per cell:

$|W\rangle = |0\rangle \qquad |B\rangle = |1\rangle \qquad |R\rangle = |+\rangle = \tfrac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \qquad |L\rangle = |-\rangle = \tfrac{|0\rangle - |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$

White and black are the computational basis. The bianconera — half white, half black, with an orientation — is an equal superposition; drawn with the white half on the right it is $|+\rangle$ ("right-oriented"), mirrored it is $|-\rangle$. The color measurement asks a superposed tile a question it does not have a definite answer to, and the Born rule replies with probabilities.

Intuition.A half-white-half-black tile has no color yet; measuring it is a fair draw between the two.
Formula.$P(W \mid R) = |\langle 0 | + \rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$.
Number.$0.500$ — the printed dice row for the bianconera is exactly 1–50 white, 51–100 black.
Play it.Start a game below, measure a board with bianconere on it, and watch the white tally converge to 50% — then remember this 50/50 is not a coin: Part 2 un-flips it.

Part 2 — The Bianconera: Why Orientation Matters

QUESTION FIRST A half-white-half-black tile turns up white exactly half the time. So does a coin. Why does the game bother printing an orientation on the tile — what could the direction of a 50/50 tile possibly change?

The move cards answer it. The card $H$ connects the two families: it turns white into a right-oriented bianconera and back, black into a left-oriented one and back. So play $H$ on a bianconera and the superposition closes:

$H|+\rangle = |0\rangle \qquad H|-\rangle = |1\rangle$
COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL One card un-flips the coin. No operation on a fair coin can make its next toss certain — a probability distribution has no handle to grab. But $H$ on a right-oriented bianconera yields a certainly white tile: 100%, not 50. The bianconera was never a distribution; it is a state with a definite orientation, and the orientation is the handle. The rulebook calls these tiles Schrödinger's kittens for exactly this reason: the superposition is a real, manipulable thing.

The same logic exposes the phase. The card $Z$ flips orientation, $Z|\pm\rangle = |\mp\rangle$, yet changes nothing a color measurement can see — both orientations measure 50/50. Invisible? Only until an $H$ converts the hidden difference into opposite certainties: $H|+\rangle = |0\rangle$ (white for sure) while $H|-\rangle = |1\rangle$ (black for sure).

COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL The $Z$ card changes nothing you can see — until it changes everything. Phase is not bookkeeping. The game tracks it with tile orientation, and one $H$ card cashes it out as opposite certain colors. This is the same relative phase that Lab 7's interferometer converted into which-detector certainty.
Intuition.The orientation is a second property, invisible to the color question but fully real.
Formula.$H|+\rangle = |0\rangle$, so $P(W \mid H \text{ on } R) = 1$.
Number.$1.000$ — not $0.500$.
Play it.In the game below: play $H$ on a bianconera, then measure. The rulebook itself recommends this tactic — it is how you build a guaranteed tris piece out of chance.

Part 3 — Entanglement: One State on Two Cells

QUESTION FIRST Two triangles on opposite corners of the board always come up the same color, every game, though each alone is a perfect coin toss. Where does the agreement live?

The two-cell card $C_X$ (a controlled-NOT: the player declares which cell is control and which is target) is the game's only source of correlation. Played with a bianconera as control and a monochrome tile as target, it entangles them into one of the four Bell states, shown on the board as a pair of triangles:

$(R, W) \to \Phi^+ \qquad (R, B) \to \Psi^+ \qquad (L, W) \to \Phi^- \qquad (L, B) \to \Psi^-$
control orientation ↦ sign of the pair  ·  target color ↦ same-color ($\Phi$) or opposite-color ($\Psi$) type

Reading order (used everywhere on this page and in the notebook): pair outcomes like $WB$ are written big-endian — the lower-numbered cell is the leftmost letter, exactly as $|q_0 q_1\rangle$ puts qubit 0 leftmost. The companion notebook shows how to convert the opposite ordering used by common circuit software.

A $\Phi^+$ pair measures $WW$ or $BB$ — never mixed — each with probability $\tfrac12$: each member alone is perfectly random, the two together perfectly agreed. The agreement does not live in either cell; it lives in the pair. Adjacency is irrelevant — cells 1 and 9 entangle as happily as neighbors. And the same card played again on the pair disentangles it back into the tiles it came from: every operation in the game is reversible; only the dice are not.

COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL Two cells that never touched agree forever. One $C_X$ card creates perfect correlation with individually perfect randomness, at any board distance. This is the same resource measured in Lab 7's entangled-photon correlations, and the resource behind quantum key distribution (Lab 6): the correlation is a property of the joint state, stored in neither place.
Intuition.The pair carries one shared answer, not two private ones.
Formula.$P(\text{same color} \mid \Phi^+) = |\langle 00|\Phi^+\rangle|^2 + |\langle 11|\Phi^+\rangle|^2 = 1$.
Number.$1.000$ agreement; the mixed outcomes have probability exactly $0$ — bands that simply do not exist on the printed row.
Play it.Below (preset +CX, the default): entangle two cells, measure many games, and watch the pair counter pin agreement at 100% while each member's own tally sits at 50/50.

Part 4 — The Pink Square: One Hidden Rotation (advanced)

QUESTION FIRST One pink square turns your certain white tile into a 25% underdog. What single operation produces 25, 75, 93 and 7 — and why does the 93 come with a $\sqrt{3}$ in it?

The rulebook never writes the pink square's matrix — it only prints its measurement table: a decorated white tile measures white 25%, decorated black 75%, decorated bianconere 93% and 7%. Those four rows pin the gate completely. A canonical rotation by angle $\theta$ sends $|0\rangle$ to $\cos\theta\,|0\rangle + \dots$, so $\cos^2\theta = \tfrac14$ forces $\theta = 60°$; matching the 93 to the right-oriented bianconera fixes the sense:

$U = R(-60°) = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & \sqrt{3}/2 \\ -\sqrt{3}/2 & 1/2 \end{pmatrix} \qquad U|+\rangle = R(-15°)\,|0\rangle \;\Rightarrow\; P(W \mid U\text{ on } R) = \cos^2 15° = \tfrac{2+\sqrt{3}}{4} \approx 0.9330$

The game's mysterious 93 is $\cos^2$ of fifteen degrees — an irrational probability, printed to two digits. One matrix reproduces every decorated row in the book, including the entangled ones with their $\tfrac{2\pm\sqrt{3}}{8}$ values (printed 47 and 3). Unlike $X$, $Y$, $Z$ and $H$, this rotation does not map the simple tile family onto itself — which is exactly why the pink square must stay on the board as a marker instead of being absorbed into a relabelled tile, and why the +U presets are the advanced tier: one little square carries the game beyond the flip-and-mix world into genuinely new probabilities.

Intuition.A rotation partway between "keep" and "flip" leaves every outcome uncertain — but by a precise, computable amount.
Formula.$P(W \mid U \text{ on } R) = \cos^2 15° = \tfrac{2+\sqrt{3}}{4}$.
Number.$0.9330127\ldots$ — printed as the 1–93 band; the print rounds the physics by $0.0030$, within its stated half-point tolerance.
Play it.Preset +U below: decorate a bianconera, roll against the printed 93/7 row, then flip to exact mode and see $0.933013$ served with all its digits.

Part 5 — Measurement: The Only Move You Can't Take Back

QUESTION FIRST You just measured the tile white. Measure its orientation, then look at the color again: half your whites turned black. Who repainted the tile?

The endgame sweep visits cells 1→9. Monochrome tiles are skipped; every superposed or decorated tile resolves by one roll against its printed row; a triangle pair resolves jointly — one roll for both cells — when the sweep first reaches it. Every resolved state is replaced by a plain, undecorated outcome tile. Sweep again and nothing changes and no dice are consumed: that is collapse, operationally — outcomes, once real, are stable.

COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL Measurement is the game's only irreversible move. Every card can be undone — $X$, $Y$, $Z$, $H$ and the pink square are their own inverses, $C_X$ un-entangles what it entangled. The dice cannot be un-rolled, and a white tile carries no memory of the bianconera it came from. Reversible dynamics, irreversible measurement: the deepest asymmetry in quantum mechanics, playable with cardboard.

The sandbox below lifts the rulebook's single-cell solitaire into a lab instrument. A color measurement asks the $|0\rangle/|1\rangle$ question. An orientation measurement asks the $|+\rangle/|-\rangle$ question, and the game offers it in two equivalent-but-different ways, named as in Lab 5: Procedure 1 projects directly onto the orientation states — the post-measurement state is an oriented bianconera tile. Procedure 2 is the rulebook's own construction: apply $H$ (the canonical rotation for this basis), measure color, and relabel the outcomes — the post-measurement state is a monochrome tile. Identical statistics, different post-states; the overlap between the two post-states is $|\langle + | 0\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$.

Color and orientation are incompatible: measuring one completely randomizes the other. Starting from a white tile, the chain orientation → color → orientation gives 50/50 at every step. Nobody repainted the tile — the color question simply has no answer while the orientation is definite, and vice versa. This is Stern-Gerlach physics: replace color/ orientation with spin-$z$/spin-$x$ and the sandbox is the sequential Stern-Gerlach experiment that today's spin-qubit hardware reads out one electron at a time.

Intuition.Two questions that cannot have simultaneous answers must randomize each other.
Formula.$|\langle 0 | + \rangle|^2 = |\langle + | 0 \rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$ — the two bases are mutually unbiased (Lab 6's key-distribution pair, in tile form).
Number.$0.500$ at every step of the chain, forever.
Play it.Open the sandbox tab below and run the guided chain; run both procedures on the same decorated tile and compare post-states.

The Erratum Panel: When Formalism Referees the Referee

This adaptation verified every printed measurement row against the underlying quantum mechanics. The result honors the game: 38 of the 40 printed rows are exact within their own stated rounding — the largest gap is half a percentage point, and the authors even rounded 12.5/37.5 in opposite directions (12 and 38) so the row totals and marginals stay perfect. Three small slips remain, declared here openly, because the game's own philosophy — the rules are the physics — deserves to be taken seriously.

COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL The printed game itself slipped — and the formalism catches it. The double-pink-square table claims two pink squares never matter on an entangled pair. That is exactly true for two of the four pair types — and provably impossible for the other two. Rotational invariance is a property of specific states, not of entanglement in general. An engine that checks every number caught a table that playtesting could not.
ERRATUM E2 — the double-U rows

The printed table for pairs with a pink square on each member shows all four pair types unchanged — perfect correlation or anticorrelation, as if the squares cancelled. For $\Phi^+$ and $\Psi^-$ this is exact physics: those two states are invariant when both members rotate together. But if the $\Phi^+$ row and the $\Phi^-$ row were both right as printed, the pink square would have to leave definite colors definite — flatly contradicting its own 25/75 row. No assignment of any rotation (indeed any unitary) to the square can make the printed table true; the obstruction is numeric and large: the relevant interference term is $\left|\,(U Z U^{T})_{01}\right|^2 = \sin^2 120° = \tfrac34$ (the engine below computes it live: …), where it would need to be $0$.

$\Phi^-$ with two U squares

Exact distributions over $(WW, WB, BW, BB)$: $\Psi^+ \to (\tfrac38, \tfrac18, \tfrac18, \tfrac38)$ and $\Phi^- \to (\tfrac18, \tfrac38, \tfrac38, \tfrac18)$ — a deviation of exactly $\tfrac38$ in every outcome from the printed rows. In this adaptation the two rows resolve by the exact eighth-partitions on an eight-sided die (the same exact-die procedure the rulebook itself provides for its other eighth-valued rows); the printed bands for these two rows are shown only here, as documentation.

Framing matters: this is a transcription slip in an otherwise remarkably accurate physical game — two rows out of forty, in the hardest table of the book. The design is sound; the formalism simply referees everyone, authors included. That is the point of formalism.

ERRATUM E1 — one number in a margin note

A collapse-stability note late in the rulebook quotes probability $\tfrac14$ for a decorated right-oriented bianconera turning up white. The book's own printed table (the 1–93 row) and the physics give $\tfrac{2+\sqrt{3}}{4} \approx 0.933$; the quoted $\tfrac14$ belongs to the decorated white tile two rows up. An authorial slip with no effect on any rule — the note's actual argument (collapsed outcomes are stable) is untouched. Recorded so nobody re-quotes the number.

ERRATUM E3 — two swapped words in one caption

One early figure caption names its right-oriented cell "left-oriented" and vice versa — the words are swapped relative to the drawing. Every other orientation-bearing figure and passage in the book (five drawings, four independent prose passages) agrees with the drawing, so the drawing is authoritative and this page's tiles follow it: right-oriented means white half on the right. Worth recording because readers comparing this page against that one caption would otherwise think the adaptation had it backwards.

Declared Deviations from the Printed Game

Everything this adaptation does differently from the physical edition, in one list:

  1. Ambiguity resolutions. Where the printed rules are silent — the exact mulligan procedure, tie handling, who is control and who is target when $C_X$ is played, how stacked markers compose, how the d100 is read, the three-pair limit — this adaptation fixes each gap physics-first, guided by the rulebook's own examples, and enforces the result consistently. Notably: the player declares control and target on every $C_X$; marker stacks compose with the pink square outermost (the unique order matching the printed 47/3 rows); ties re-roll; equal scores are a draw.
  2. Exact mode. The physical game rolls d100 against printed integer bands. This adaptation adds an optional exact mode that samples the true probabilities in full precision — and documents that on 38 of 40 rows the printed bands sit within 0.005 of the truth. The printed-tables mode remains the default and reproduces the published bands verbatim.
  3. English translation. The original is Italian; this page is English with the glossary above. Rule semantics are unchanged in translation.
  4. A seam for a computer opponent. The engine is a pure state machine with no assumptions about who chooses moves, so a vs-computer mode can attach later. It is deliberately not implemented here: hot-seat play between two humans is the mode the original game is designed around.
  5. The two erratum rows are not playable as printed. The double-U rows for $\Psi^+$ and $\Phi^-$ resolve by the exact eighth-partitions in both dice modes; the impossible printed bands appear only in the erratum panel above. Serving the physics — openly — was judged more faithful to the game's philosophy than serving the typo.

Exercises

Q1 — The un-flippable coin

A right-oriented bianconera is on cell 5. Which single card makes the upcoming color measurement certain, and with which outcome?

Q2 — Reading the pair table

A $\Psi^+$ pair (no markers) is measured. What is the probability that the two cells come up the same color?

Q3 — Where the √3 lives

$P(W)$ for a pink-decorated right-oriented bianconera is $\cos^2 15°$. Which printed band encodes it, and how far is the print from the physics?

Q4 — The erratum argument

Why can no choice of gate for the pink square make the printed double-U table right?

Play-tasks (open-ended, at the board)
  • P1. Starting from a white and a black tile, prepare the $\Psi^-$ pair using only cards. (One solution uses two cards; check yourself with the probability preview.)
  • P2. Make the erratum visible: in preset +U+CX, build a $\Phi^-$ pair, decorate both members with pink squares, and measure many times. Compare your tallies with the printed row shown in the erratum panel and with the exact eighths.
  • P3. In the sandbox, find a state for which Procedures 1 and 2 give different statistics. (Spoiler: you can't — that is the theorem. Then find what does differ.)
