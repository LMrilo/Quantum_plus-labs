Lab 7: Quantum Optics

Single Photons, Interference, and Entangled Light — the Hardware of Quantum Technology

Prerequisites: Fundamental Concepts of Quantum Technologies & Labs 5–6 1 ECTS ≈ 10 h lab work Mode: Interactive Optical Bench + Qiskit

Rationale

Labs 5 and 6 gave you the mathematics of qubits and a protocol built on it. This lab shows you the hardware those abstractions run on. A single photon is a qubit — twice over: its path through an interferometer is one two-level system, its polarization is another. Every device on the optical bench — beam splitter, phase shifter, polarizer — is one of the operators you already know, executed by glass and crystal. And every strange number this lab produces (a coincidence counter pinned at zero, two 50/50 elements composing to certainty, a filter that revives blocked light, twin photons that always agree) is a Born-rule computation you have already done abstractly.

LAB 5–6 BRIDGE The path states $|A\rangle, |B\rangle$ and the polarization states $|H\rangle, |V\rangle$ are both the Lab 5 computational basis; the diagonal polarizations at ±45° are exactly the Lab 5 X-basis states $|{+}\rangle, |{-}\rangle$. The four BB84 signal states of Lab 6 are physically $|H\rangle, |V\rangle, |{+}\rangle, |{-}\rangle$ — Part C of this lab is the transmitter and receiver hardware of a real QKD link, and Part B's which-path result is Lab 6's "Eve leaves fingerprints" theorem restated as an identity about interference.

Part A: One Photon at a Beam Splitter

RUN THIS FIRST Open the optical bench below in Preset A: a single-photon source, one 50/50 beam splitter, a detector on each output port, and a coincidence counter wired to both. Fire 10 000 photons and watch two things: the two detector tallies, and the coincidence counter. The tallies drift toward 50/50 — but the coincidence counter never moves. Keep that second observation in mind; it is the whole section.
What does it actually mean that light comes in photons — and what happens when one indivisible photon hits a half-silvered mirror?
COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL One photon takes both paths — the state after the beam splitter is a superposition over the two output arms, and Part B will prove it by interference — yet it never triggers both detectors. Not rarely: never. The photon is divisible in amplitude but indivisible in detection.

A half-silvered mirror (a 50/50 beam splitter) transmits half the light and reflects half. For a classical wave that sentence is unambiguous: the energy splits, half into each arm. But light arrives in indivisible quanta. A single photon cannot deliver half a click to each detector — so what does "splits 50/50" mean for one photon?

Quantum mechanics answers with a state, not a story. Write the two output paths as the basis states $|A\rangle$ (transmitted) and $|B\rangle$ (reflected) — the same two-level system as any Lab 5 qubit. A lossless beam splitter must act as a unitary on this qubit (unitarity here is energy conservation — the photon must exit somewhere), and the symmetric 50/50 choice is:

$$B = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad B|A\rangle = \frac{|A\rangle + i\,|B\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$$
Why the $i$? A naive "50/50 without phase", $\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, is a singular matrix — it is not a possible physical device, because it would merge two distinguishable inputs into one output and lose a photon in the process. The phase $i$ on reflection is the minimal repair that restores unitarity. This phase convention ($i$ on reflection, used consistently) is fixed for the entire lab — every formula below depends on it, and the simulator uses exactly this matrix.

Detection at the two output ports is a path-basis measurement — Procedure 1 from Lab 5, executed destructively (the photon is absorbed by the detector that clicks). The Born rule gives the two outcome probabilities:

$$P(\text{detector A}) = \left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\right|^2 = \frac12, \qquad P(\text{detector B}) = \left|\tfrac{i}{\sqrt 2}\right|^2 = \frac12,$$ $$P(\text{both detectors}) = 0 \;\;\text{exactly.}$$

That last line deserves a hard look. The state $(|A\rangle + i|B\rangle)/\sqrt 2$ is one photon in superposition over two paths — not two photons. A path measurement has exactly two mutually exclusive outcomes; "both detectors click" is not a low-probability event, it is not in the outcome set at all. The zero is structural, not statistical.

Formula → number → bench. $P = \tfrac12, \tfrac12$ and $P_{\text{coinc}} = 0$. In Preset A: after 10 000 photons the tallies land within statistical noise of 5 000 / 5 000 (the theory overlay shows the exact 50% line), while the coincidence counter reads 0 — exactly, after every single photon. Fire another 10 000 and check that the zero is not luck.
The honest classical contrast. A classical wave or pulse really does split: half the energy in each arm, and with two detectors both can fire in the same trial — semiclassical models predict coincidences at least at the random-chance level. The quantum prediction is zero. Grangier, Roger and Aspect measured exactly this anti-correlation in 1986; it is the operational meaning of "the photon is indivisible", and it is the first of this lab's four experimental pillars.
Where this is an industry, not a thought experiment. One photon distributed over two spatial modes is the dual-rail qubit — the native encoding of photonic quantum computing. Quandela builds the single-photon sources (the "fire one photon on demand" box the simulator idealizes) and dual-rail photonic processors around exactly this $\{|A\rangle, |B\rangle\}$ qubit; ORCA Computing builds photonic quantum processors on the same principle that beam-splitter networks perform unitary computation on light. The linear-optical quantum computing literature (KLM protocol) showed such networks plus detection are universal. When you fire photons at the bench below, you are operating the elementary cell of those machines.

Part B: The Mach–Zehnder Interferometer and Which-Path Information

RUN THIS FIRST Switch the bench to Preset B1: two beam splitters back to back, mirrors folding the two arms together, and a phase knob $\varphi$ on arm B. Leave $\varphi = 0°$ and fire 10 000 photons. Every photon exits at D1 — 100/0, from a device built of two 50/50 elements. Now sweep the $\varphi$ slider and watch the split move. The counts depend on the phase difference between the two paths.
If the photon went one way or the other, the detector counts could not depend on the path it didn't take. They do. What now?
COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL Two 50/50 beam splitters in sequence give a 100/0 outcome. Model each splitter as an independent coin flip — the reasonable move if the photon "really" takes one path — and you predict 50/50 at the output. The observed perfect nulls and perfect peaks are the single-photon interference signature: the amplitudes of both paths add before anything is detected.

The Mach–Zehnder interferometer is the workhorse: beam splitter BS1 opens two paths, mirrors bring them back together, a phase shifter $\Phi(\varphi) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\varphi})$ on arm B models any path-length difference, and beam splitter BS2 recombines. (The mirrors add the same phase to both arms — a global phase, dropped.) Step the input $|A\rangle$ through the device:

$$|A\rangle \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{BS1}\;}\; \frac{|A\rangle + i|B\rangle}{\sqrt 2} \;\xrightarrow{\;\Phi(\varphi)\;}\; \frac{|A\rangle + i e^{i\varphi}|B\rangle}{\sqrt 2} \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{BS2}\;}\; \frac{(1 - e^{i\varphi})|A\rangle + i(1 + e^{i\varphi})|B\rangle}{2}$$

With detector D1 on output port B and D2 on output port A, the Born rule and the identity $|1 \pm e^{i\varphi}|^2 = 2 \pm 2\cos\varphi$ give the fringes:

$$P(D1) = \cos^2\!\frac{\varphi}{2}, \qquad P(D2) = \sin^2\!\frac{\varphi}{2}$$

At $\varphi = 0$: $P(D1) = 1$ — the 100/0 you just observed. Algebraically this is the composition identity $B^2 = iX$: two symmetric beam splitters are a perfect path swap (up to an overall phase). At $\varphi = \pi$ the same instrument, retuned, sends every photon to D2 instead. Between the extremes the split interpolates as a perfect cosine — fringe visibility $\mathcal{V} = (P_{\max} - P_{\min})/(P_{\max} + P_{\min}) = 1$.

Formula → number → bench. At $\varphi = \pi/3$ (60°): $P(D1) = \cos^2(30°) = \tfrac34$ — a 75% / 25% split. In Preset B1, set the slider to 60° and fire 10 000 photons: the tallies land on 75/25 within noise, on top of the theory overlay. Then return to $\varphi = 0°$ and re-verify the 100/0 — two 50/50 splitters, certain outcome.
The fringe as a measuring instrument. $\varphi$ is optical path-length difference in disguise. Any physical quantity that stretches one arm — rotation in a fiber-optic gyroscope (the navigation-grade rotation sensors in aircraft), strain, temperature, or a passing gravitational wave in the kilometer-scale LIGO-class interferometers — shifts the fringe. Near the steepest point of the cosine, reading $P(D1)$ to precision $\delta P$ reads the phase to $\delta\varphi \approx 2\,\delta P$: counting photons is the measurement. That is interferometric sensing, and it is this exact circuit.

Which-path marking: complementarity, quantified

RUN THIS FIRST Switch to Preset B2: the same interferometer, plus a which-path marker in the arms — a probe qubit that records which arm the photon took. Its dial is the marker overlap $s$: at $s = 1$ the marker states for the two arms are identical (no record); at $s = 0$ they are orthogonal (a perfect record). Set $s = 0$, sweep $\varphi$, and watch the fringes: they are gone — flat 50/50 at every phase. Now slide $s$ upward and watch them come back, exactly in proportion.
COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL An unread which-path marker still destroys the fringes. Nobody looks at the record. It is enough that the record exists — that the universe could in principle tell which path was taken. Interference is lost the moment the paths become distinguishable, not the moment someone learns the answer.

Model the marking honestly. Between the beam splitters, couple the path qubit to a marker qubit: path A tags the marker into state $|M_A\rangle$, path B into $|M_B\rangle$. We write the two-qubit state as $|\text{path}\,\text{marker}\rangle$ with the path qubit leftmost — big-endian ordering, qubit 0 is the leftmost symbol (the companion notebook converts Qiskit's opposite bitstring order before displaying anything). After BS1 and the marking:

$$\frac{|A\rangle|M_A\rangle + i e^{i\varphi}\,|B\rangle|M_B\rangle}{\sqrt 2}$$

Recombine at BS2 and compute $P(D1)$, summing over the marker (read or unread — the arithmetic cannot tell). Everything collapses to one parameter, the overlap $s = \langle M_A|M_B\rangle$:

$$P(D1;\varphi, s) = \frac{1 + s\cos\varphi}{2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\mathcal{V} = s = |\langle M_A|M_B\rangle|\;}$$

Fringe visibility equals the indistinguishability of the marker states. $s = 1$: no record, full fringes. $s = 0$: perfect record, no fringes — $P(D1) = \tfrac12$ at every phase, as if the photon really had flipped a coin at BS1. In between, the trade is exactly linear in the fringe term. A perfect marking ($s = 0$) is physically a controlled-NOT: the path controls the marker, and reading the marker afterwards is a genuine path measurement (Procedure 1 on the marker qubit) — same statistics as putting a detector in the arm, but the photon survives to reach BS2.

Formula → number → bench. At $s = 0.5$: $\mathcal{V} = 0.5$, so the fringes run between $P_{\max} = \tfrac{1+0.5}{2} = \mathbf{0.75}$ and $P_{\min} = \tfrac{1-0.5}{2} = \mathbf{0.25}$. In Preset B2, set $s = 0.5$, fire at $\varphi = 0°$ (→ 75%) and at $\varphi = 180°$ (→ 25%), and read the visibility card: it converges to 0.5 — the dial you set, recovered from counting statistics alone.
LAB 5–6 BRIDGE Lab 5 said "measurement disturbs the state"; Lab 6 said "Eve's measurement leaves fingerprints". Both are this one identity $\mathcal{V} = |\langle M_A|M_B\rangle|$: an eavesdropper who gains path information ($s < 1$) necessarily degrades the interference any honest party could observe. Information out ⟹ coherence lost, in exact proportion. (The general form is Englert's duality relation $\mathcal{V}^2 + \mathcal{D}^2 \le 1$ with distinguishability $\mathcal{D} = \sqrt{1 - s^2}$ — our ideal marker saturates it.)

Part C: Polarization — Quantum Malus and the Three-Polarizer Surprise

RUN THIS FIRST Switch to Preset C: an unpolarized source and a rack of polarizing filters. Start with two crossed polarizers — 0° and 90°. Fire 10 000 photons: nothing gets through. Now tick "insert 45° between them" and fire again: 12.5% of the photons now pass all three filters. You added an absorber, and the light came back.
Two crossed filters block everything. Insert a third filter between them and light comes through. Why does adding an absorber increase transmission?
COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL Adding an absorbing polarizer raises transmission from exactly 0 to 1/8. No sieve, filter, or "the polarizer lets through the right components" picture survives this: a filter that only ever removes light cannot make more light arrive. Something other than removal is happening. (You can try this at home with three lenses from polarized sunglasses.)

Polarization is the photon's second qubit: $|H\rangle$ (horizontal) and $|V\rangle$ (vertical) form the computational basis, and the state at linear angle $\theta$ from the H-axis is $|\theta\rangle = \cos\theta\,|H\rangle + \sin\theta\,|V\rangle$. The diagonal states at ±45° are exactly the Lab 5 X-basis pair $|{\pm}\rangle$.

An ideal polarizer at angle $\theta$ is the projector $\Pi_\theta = |\theta\rangle\langle\theta|$, and this is the crucial fact: a polarizer is an inherently Procedure-1 device. Each photon either passes — and is then in state $|\theta\rangle$ — or is absorbed. There is no fractional photon. For a photon in $|H\rangle$ meeting a polarizer at $\theta$, the Born rule gives the quantum Malus law:

$$P(\text{pass}) = |\langle\theta|H\rangle|^2 = \cos^2\theta, \qquad \text{post-state } |\theta\rangle$$
polarizer angle $\theta$0°30°45°60°90°
$P(\text{pass})$ from $|H\rangle$ 13/41/21/4 0

Classical Malus ($I = I_0\cos^2\theta$) reads identically — but the quantum version is per-photon and probabilistic: $\cos^2\theta$ is a Born probability, the same $|\langle m|\psi\rangle|^2$ as every measurement since Lab 5, and each individual photon entirely passes or is entirely absorbed.

Formula → number → bench. $\cos^2(60°) = \mathbf{25\%}$. In Preset C, switch the source to $|H\rangle$, enable a single polarizer at 60°, and fire 10 000 photons: the pass fraction lands on 25% within noise. Then rebuild the crossed pair (unpolarized source, 0° and 90°) → exactly 0 — and insert the 45° filter → 1/8 = 12.5%.

Now the resolution of the three-polarizer surprise. For a cascade $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_N$, each passage renews the state to the current polarizer's $|\theta_j\rangle$ — the Procedure-1 post-state. So the pass probabilities multiply with the differences between consecutive angles:

$$P(\text{pass all}) = P_1 \cdot \prod_{j=2}^{N}\cos^2(\theta_j - \theta_{j-1})$$

with $P_1 = \tfrac12$ for an unpolarized source (no polarization preference — every first filter passes half). The two experiments you ran:

$$\text{crossed } (0°, 90°):\;\; \tfrac12\cdot\cos^2(90°) = 0 \qquad\qquad \text{three } (0°, 45°, 90°):\;\; \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac18$$

The middle filter does not "let more light through" — it rewrites the state. After the 45° polarizer the photon is in $|{+}\rangle$, which is no longer orthogonal to the final 90° filter. The projective post-state — the fact that measurement changes the system, the disturbance you met in Lab 5 — is the entire explanation. The 45° polarizer is an X-basis measurement of the polarization qubit, executed by a plastic film.

Procedure 1 vs Procedure 2 — two physically different devices. Lab 5 distinguished two ways to measure in the diagonal basis, and in optics they are different hardware:
  • Procedure 1 — a polarizer at 45°: probabilities $|\langle\pm|\psi\rangle|^2$, passed photon left in $|{+}\rangle$ (the $|{-}\rangle$ outcome is an absorbed photon).
  • Procedure 2 — a half-waveplate at 22.5° (which implements the canonical basis-change rotation $U_X = H$) followed by a polarizing beam splitter and two detectors: identical probabilities, but the photon exits in $|H\rangle$ or $|V\rangle$, not $|{\pm}\rangle$.
Same statistics, different post-measurement states: the overlap between the two devices' "+" outputs is $|\langle + | H\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$, not 1. Downstream optics see different photons. Lab 6's naive-resend attack — where Eve's forgotten re-encoding turned a 25% error rate into 37.5% — was exactly this distinction carrying a security consequence.
This hardware runs QKD networks today. Polarization-encoded BB84 uses precisely these components: Lab 6's four signal states are $|H\rangle, |V\rangle, |{+}\rangle, |{-}\rangle$; Alice's transmitter is a polarization preparer, Bob's receiver is a basis-switched analyzer — a rotation plus a polarizing beam splitter, Procedure 2 in glass. Metropolitan QKD fiber networks (deployed in Europe and Asia, and the building block of the planned EuroQCI infrastructure) run this lab's Part C optics at telecom scale: every "secure key bit" started life as a photon meeting a polarization analyzer.
Deep dive — the polarizer staircase. Generalize the insertion trick: between 0° and 90°, insert $N-1$ intermediate filters in equal steps of $90°/N$ (so $N+1$ polarizers in total, counting both ends). The transmission $\tfrac12\cos^{2N}(90°/N)$ rises toward $\tfrac12$ as $N$ grows — many gentle projections approximate a lossless rotation of the polarization. Try $0°, 30°, 60°, 90°$ in Preset C: $\tfrac12(3/4)^3 = 27/128 \approx 21\%$, already far above the 12.5% of the single insertion.

Part D: Entangled Photon Pairs OPTIONAL

This part is optional enrichment: nothing in the exercises or the notebook's core sections depends on it. It is also the doorway to the deepest experimental result in the field — read on if you want to see it.

RUN THIS FIRST Switch to Preset D: a crystal that emits pairs of photons, one to each side, each side ending in an analyzer (angle dial + two detectors, "+" and "−"). Set both dials to the same angle — any angle — and fire 10 000 pairs. Each side alone clicks "+" or "−" like a fair coin. But the agreement counter reads 100%: the two sides always give the same answer. Now change the common angle and fire again — still 100%.
Two photons, each individually a perfect coin toss, always agree when asked the same question — at any angle, chosen after they separated. What kind of correlation is that?
COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL Entangled photons are individually random at every angle, perfectly correlated whenever the angles match — and the best classical wave model is not just vaguely wrong about this, it is off by exactly a factor of 2 in the correlation amplitude. The simulator plots both curves; the gap between them is the measurement.

Nonlinear crystals (spontaneous parametric down-conversion — the workhorse entangled-pair source since the 1990s) emit two photons in the joint polarization state

$$|\Phi^{+}\rangle = \frac{|HH\rangle + |VV\rangle}{\sqrt 2}$$

written photon 1 leftmost, photon 2 rightmost (the same leftmost-first, big-endian ordering as Part B's $|\text{path}\,\text{marker}\rangle$ kets). Each side passes a two-channel analyzer at angle $\alpha$ (photon 1) or $\beta$ (photon 2): outcome "+" means the photon exited the $|\theta_{\text{analyzer}}\rangle$ port, "−" the orthogonal port. Two facts, both exact:

Each photon alone: a perfect coin. Summing over the partner's outcomes leaves photon 1 (or 2) in the maximally mixed state — exactly unpolarized. $P({+}) = \tfrac12$ at every analyzer angle. No analyzer setting on one side is visible in the other side's single rates.
The pair together: locked. With $\Delta = \alpha - \beta$: $$P({+}{+}) = P({-}{-}) = \frac{\cos^2\Delta}{2}, \quad P({+}{-}) = P({-}{+}) = \frac{\sin^2\Delta}{2}$$ At matched angles ($\Delta = 0$): agreement with certainty.

The standard summary is the correlation function $E(\alpha,\beta) = P({+}{+}) + P({-}{-}) - P({+}{-}) - P({-}{+})$, which collapses to a function of the angle difference alone — the state $|\Phi^{+}\rangle$ is invariant when both analyzers rotate together:

$$E(\alpha, \beta) = \cos 2\Delta, \qquad \Delta = \alpha - \beta$$
Formula → number → bench. $E(0°, 22.5°) = \cos 45° = \tfrac{\sqrt 2}{2} \approx \mathbf{0.707}$. In Preset D, set $\alpha = 0°, \beta = 22.5°$, fire 10 000 pairs, and read the measured $E$ against the theory overlay. Then set the dials equal ($\Delta = 0$) and confirm 100% agreement — against the classical-wave overlay's 75% — while each side's own counter stays at 50/50. At $\Delta = 45°$, $E = 0$: the two analyzers have become mutually unbiased — Lab 6's key concept reappearing as zero correlation.

Why is this deep and not just pretty? Try the obvious classical story: each pair is emitted with a shared random polarization $\lambda$, and each analyzer responds by classical Malus, $\cos^2$ of the angle to $\lambda$. Average over $\lambda$ and the model produces a correlation of the same shape — but half the size:

$$E_{\text{cl}}(\Delta) = \frac{\cos 2\Delta}{2}$$

At matched analyzers the classical wave model manages only 75% agreement ($E_{\text{cl}}(0) = \tfrac12$) against the observed 100%. This is not a philosophical complaint — it is a device-verifiable factor of 2, and the simulator's overlay draws both curves so you can watch the measured points choose the quantum one. More sophisticated classical models (predetermined answers for every angle) can patch the matched-angle agreement but then fail elsewhere on the $\cos 2\Delta$ curve — that is Bell's theorem, which we cite rather than derive: no local classical story reproduces the full curve. What this lab establishes at the correlation level is the honest core: the measured curve is $\cos 2\Delta$, and the classical wave prediction misses it by a factor of 2.

Entanglement as infrastructure. The pair source you are firing is the physical basis of entanglement-based QKD (the BBM92 protocol): Alice and Bob each receive one photon of a pair, measure in randomly switched bases, and the matched-angle perfect correlations play the role that Alice's prepared states played in Lab 6's BB84 — with the added property that the correlations themselves certify that no third party is entangled with the key. The same correlations, measured across 1 200 km by the Micius satellite, are the record distance for entanglement distribution — Part D at continental scale.
