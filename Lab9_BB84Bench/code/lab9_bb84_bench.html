1 · Kit anatomy

What will be on the table?

BB84 (Bennett and Brassard, 1984) is a protocol for quantum key distribution (QKD): two parties, Alice and Bob, build a shared secret key, a string of random bits that only they know, and can tell whether an eavesdropper, Eve, listened in. It is not only a classroom protocol: the Micius satellite distributed BB84 keys from space to the ground in 2017, and EuroQCI is the European Commission's initiative for a quantum communication infrastructure across the EU. At i-LAMP you run BB84 on the Thorlabs EDU-QCRY1 kit. These are the words you will need at the bench:

Pulse
A short flash of red laser light. Alice sends one pulse per bit.
Polarization
The direction in which the light's electric field oscillates, given as an angle. Horizontal (0°) is written $|H\rangle$ and vertical (90°) $|V\rangle$; as bits, $|H\rangle = |0\rangle$ and $|V\rangle = |1\rangle$.
Half-wave plate ($\lambda/2$ plate)
A thin crystal plate that turns the polarization. Each kit plate sits in a mount with a dial; the dial reads the setting in degrees.
Polarizing beam splitter (PBS)
A glass cube that lets horizontally polarized light straight through (transmitted) and sends vertically polarized light off to the side (reflected). Light at any other angle is shared between the two exits.
Sensor
A detector with a blue LED that lights when a pulse arrives. Bob has two: one on each exit of his PBS.
Basis
A pair of perpendicular polarizations. The $+$ basis is {0°, 90°}: 0° is bit 0, 90° is bit 1. The $\times$ basis is {−45°, 45°}: on the kit, the "−45" setting is bit 0 and the "45" setting is bit 1. Which diagonal is called 0 is a naming convention with no physical effect.

A small electronics box reads Bob's two sensors. Its green button switches between two modes, and a side LED tells you which one is on (manual §7.2.3). They differ only when a pulse is shared equally between the two sensors, as when Alice's and Bob's bases differ:

Adjustment mode (side LED yellow): an equal split lights both blue LEDs. Used to check the alignment.
Measuring mode (side LED green): an equal split lights one blue LED, picked at random by the electronics. Used for the key runs.
DECLARED DEVIATION · DEV-9.1 Bright pulses and an electronic coin. BB84 is meant to run on single photons. The kit sends bright pulses of ordinary laser light instead. When a pulse reaches the PBS polarized halfway between horizontal and vertical, it really does split, half its light to each sensor: that is what adjustment mode shows. In measuring mode the electronics then picks one LED at random, like a coin toss. A single photon would not split; it would turn up at one sensor or the other, and nothing on the bench would decide in advance which. The odds are the same, but the coin is electronic and every result can be explained with classical light (manual Ch. 3). The kit is an analogy of BB84, not a quantum channel.
2 · Safety

Is it safe?

Yes, if everyone at the table follows these rules.

  • The lasers are class 2: red light at 635 nm, in pulses of at most 1 mW (manual Ch. 13). Class 2 means no safety goggles are needed, as long as nobody stares into the beam.
  • Never look into the beam or its reflections. If the beam catches your eye, close your eyes and turn your head away on purpose; do not count on blinking (manual Ch. 2, Ch. 13).
  • Do not lean on or bump the table. It shifts the lasers and sensors out of alignment (manual §7.4), and a misaligned bench produces errors that look like an eavesdropper.
  • The staff align the kit. Do not move the mounts, and do not touch the glass plates: they are handled only with gloves (manual Ch. 2). If a check fails, call the staff.
3 · Predictions

What do you expect?

Commit to an answer before you open each one; at the bench you will check it. All answers assume an ideal bench: perfect plates and sensors, no light lost, no noise. A real bench is slightly misaligned and can show a few errors even without Eve; spotting that is part of the lab.

P1.Call $s_A$ and $s_B$ the settings on Alice's and Bob's dials. The bench guide (Part A) gives the rule: a pulse is transmitted with probability $\cos^2(s_A + s_B)$ and reflected with probability $\sin^2(s_A + s_B)$. Alice sets 45°, Bob sets 45°. Which sensor lights up?
Show the answer
$\cos^2(45^\circ + 45^\circ) = \cos^2 90^\circ = 0$: the pulse is never transmitted, always reflected. Sensor 1 lights: bit 1.
When Alice's and Bob's bases differ, the formula gives $\tfrac12$ instead: the pulse splits equally between the two sensors. In adjustment mode both LEDs light; in measuring mode one lights at random.
P2.Before each pulse, Alice rolls a die to pick her basis ($+$ or $\times$) and her bit; Bob rolls another die to pick his basis. On what fraction of the pulses do their bases match?
Show the answer
One half: both pick $+$ with probability $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12$, both pick $\times$ with probability $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12$, and $\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$. Only these pulses are kept. This step is called sifting: Alice and Bob announce their bases in public (never their bits) and drop the pulses where the bases differed. From the kit's 52 pulses, expect about 26 sifted bits.
P3.The QBER (quantum bit error rate) is the fraction of sifted bits where Bob's bit differs from Alice's. In an intercept-resend attack, Eve catches each pulse, reads it in a basis she picks at random, and sends Bob a fresh pulse carrying the bit she read, in her basis. What QBER do you expect (a) without Eve, (b) with Eve?
Show the answer
(a) Zero: with matching bases, Bob reads exactly Alice's bit (P1: the certain cases).
(b) 25%. Half the time Eve picks Alice's basis, reads Alice's bit and resends it faithfully: no error. The other half she picks the wrong basis: her bit is a coin flip, her pulse arrives in the wrong basis, and Bob's bit is a coin flip too, wrong half the time. So $Q = \tfrac12\cdot 0 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14$. The errors sit only on pulses where Eve's basis differed from Alice's.
P4.To look for Eve, Alice and Bob compare 6 of their sifted bits in public, the test bits, and then drop them from the key. Suppose Eve intercepted every pulse. What is the chance that all 6 test bits still agree, so that Eve goes unnoticed?
Show the answer
Each test bit exposes Eve with probability $\tfrac14$ (the QBER of P3), so it lets her through with probability $\tfrac34$. Six independent test bits all let her through with probability $(3/4)^6$, about 17.8%. Eve slips past a 6-bit test surprisingly often; Part D of the bench guide shows how many test bits make her escape rare, and what they cost in pulses.
4 · Playbox

Are you a good random number generator?

THE QUESTION At the bench every basis and every bit comes from dice. Could your head do the dice's job? Write 52 bits as if you were the dice, then see how they compare with a fair source.

A run of 1s is a block of consecutive 1s with a 0, or the end of the string, on each side: 0111010 has two runs, of length 3 and 1. For a fair source (every bit 0 or 1 with probability ½, independently of the others) the chart shows the expected number of runs of each length: the average over many repetitions, not what every single string must contain.

Intuition.For a run of exactly $n$ ones to start somewhere in the middle of the string, $n + 2$ bits must come out right: a 0, then $n$ ones, then a 0. Each fair bit comes out right with probability ½, so every extra 1 in the run halves its chance.
Formula.Counting every place a run can start (at the two ends of the string one fewer bit has to match), the expected number of runs of length exactly $n < L$ in $L$ fair bits is $$\frac{L - n + 3}{2^{\,n+2}},$$ and $2^{-L}$ for $n = L$ (the all-ones string). The expected total number of runs is $(L + 1)/4$. As $L$ grows, the fraction of runs that have length $n$ tends to $2^{-n}$: half of all runs are single 1s, a quarter are pairs, an eighth are triples, and so on.

Runs of 0s obey exactly the same law: swap every 0 and 1 and nothing in the argument changes.

COUNTERINTUITIVE — AND REAL Long runs look non-random, so people leave them out. Asked to write random bits, people rarely write a run longer than 5, because it does not look random (manual Ch. 10). A fair source writes them anyway: in 52 fair bits, expect on average … runs of 1s of length 5 or more, … of them longer than 5, and as many runs of 0s. Across many strings, too few long runs gives a human writer away. That is why the kit ships dice (a black 8-sided die for Alice's basis and bit, a red 6-sided die for Bob's basis; manual Ch. 10) and why the bench guide says use the dice, not your head. In BB84 it matters: if Alice's choices are predictable, Eve guesses her basis more often than half the time, and fewer of her interceptions leave errors behind.
REAL-WORLD ANCHOR Real QKD systems draw their basis and bit choices from quantum random number generators (QRNGs): devices whose bits come from a quantum measurement, for example a single photon meeting a beam splitter, where nothing decides in advance which exit it takes. The kit manual (Ch. 10) names ID Quantique's Quantis as a commercial example.
