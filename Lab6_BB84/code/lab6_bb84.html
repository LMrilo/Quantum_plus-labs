Lab 6: Quantum Key Distribution

The BB84 Protocol — Measurement as a Security Primitive

Prerequisites: Lab 5 (Quantum Measurements) & Quantum Communication course 1 ECTS ≈ 10 h lab work Mode: Interactive Simulator + Qiskit

Rationale

In Lab 5 you learned that measuring a qubit in the wrong basis gives a random outcome and irreversibly disturbs the state. That looked like a limitation. In 1984, Bennett and Brassard turned it into a feature: if an eavesdropper cannot measure without disturbing, then disturbance is evidence of eavesdropping — and two parties can build a shared secret key whose security rests on physics, not on computational hardness.

LAB 5 BRIDGE Everything in BB84 is applied Lab 5. The four signal states are exactly the states $|0\rangle, |1\rangle, |{+}\rangle, |{-}\rangle$ you placed on the Bloch sphere; the measurement statistics are the Born rule you derived; and the distinction between Procedure 1 (direct basis projection) and Procedure 2 (rotate with $H$, then Z-measure) — which seemed academic — becomes operationally decisive when the eavesdropper must resend a quantum state (Part 4).

Part 1: The Four BB84 States

Alice encodes each random bit $a \in \{0,1\}$ in one of two randomly chosen bases: the Z-basis (rectilinear, +) or the X-basis (diagonal, ×). The encoding rule: bit 0 always maps to the $+1$ eigenstate of the basis, bit 1 to the $-1$ eigenstate.

Encoding map (photon-polarization picture in parentheses):
$|\psi(0,Z)\rangle = |0\rangle$ (vertical ↑)   $|\psi(1,Z)\rangle = |1\rangle$ (horizontal →)   $|\psi(0,X)\rangle = |{+}\rangle$ (diagonal ⤢)   $|\psi(1,X)\rangle = |{-}\rangle$ (diagonal ⤡)
with $|{\pm}\rangle = (|0\rangle \pm |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ — on the Lab 5 Bloch sphere: the poles ($\theta = 0, \pi$) and two equator points ($\theta = \pi/2$, $\varphi = 0, \pi$).

The two bases are mutually unbiased: writing $|z_0\rangle = |0\rangle, |z_1\rangle = |1\rangle, |x_0\rangle = |{+}\rangle, |x_1\rangle = |{-}\rangle$,

$$|\langle z_j | x_l \rangle|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \quad \text{for all } j, l \in \{0,1\}.$$
Why this is the security foundation: a measurement in the wrong basis yields a uniformly random outcome — it extracts zero information about the encoded bit and (Lab 5!) destroys the original state in the process. Everything that follows is this one fact, exploited systematically.

Part 2: Measurement Statistics — the Born Table

Applying the Born rule $P(m \,|\, \psi; B) = |\langle m | \psi \rangle|^2$ from Lab 5 to the four BB84 states in both bases gives the complete statistics of the protocol:

State sent Z-measurement: $P(0)$, $P(1)$ X-measurement: $P(+)$, $P(-)$
$|0\rangle$ 10 1/21/2
$|1\rangle$ 01 1/21/2
$|{+}\rangle$ 1/21/2 10
$|{-}\rangle$ 1/21/2 01

green matched basis — deterministic recovery of the bit;   amber mismatched basis — coin-flip outcome, bit erased.

Procedure 1 vs Procedure 2 — read this carefully. The table above lists probabilities, and these are identical whether the X-basis measurement is done by Procedure 1 (direct projection onto $\{|{+}\rangle, |{-}\rangle\}$) or by Procedure 2 (apply the canonical rotation $U_X = H$, then Z-measure — what real hardware does). Lab 5 established this equivalence. Bob only keeps a classical bit, so for Bob the choice of procedure is invisible: with Procedure 2 the Z-outcome $c \in \{0,1\}$ maps to bit $b = c$ because $H|{+}\rangle = |0\rangle$ and $H|{-}\rangle = |1\rangle$.

But the two procedures leave different post-measurement states (Procedure 1: a basis eigenstate; Procedure 2: $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$). Bob never cares. The eavesdropper will — she must resend a quantum state, and a post-measurement state is exactly what she has in hand. Hold that thought for Part 4.

Part 3: The Protocol, Sifting, and the Error Check

For each round $i = 1, \dots, n$:

  1. Alice draws a uniform random bit $a_i$ and basis $\alpha_i \in \{Z, X\}$, prepares $|\psi(a_i, \alpha_i)\rangle$, and sends it through the quantum channel.
  2. Bob draws his own uniform random basis $\beta_i \in \{Z, X\}$ — independently, since he cannot know $\alpha_i$ — and measures, recording bit $b_i$. (On hardware: Procedure 2 with $U_X = H$.)
  3. Basis reconciliation: over an authenticated public channel, Alice and Bob announce their bases — never the bits. They keep the sifted set $S = \{i : \alpha_i = \beta_i\}$ and discard everything else.
  4. Error check: they sacrifice a random subset of $k$ sifted positions, publicly compare $a_i$ vs $b_i$ there, and estimate the quantum bit error rate (QBER). Too many errors → abort.
  5. Key: the remaining sifted, unchecked bits form the raw shared key.
Sifted fraction. $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ are independent and uniform on two values, so $$P(\alpha_i = \beta_i) = P(Z,Z) + P(X,X) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12 .$$ On average half the rounds survive sifting: expected sifted key length $n/2$.
QBER without eavesdropping. On an ideal channel, every sifted round is a matched-basis measurement — the green cells of the Born table. Those are deterministic, so $$Q_{\text{no Eve}} = P(b_i \neq a_i \mid i \in S) = 0 \quad \text{exactly.}$$ Not "small" — zero. On an ideal channel, any error in the sifted key is evidence that something interfered with the qubits in transit.
Why sifting matters: without step 3, mismatched-basis rounds (coin flips) would contribute errors even with no eavesdropper at all — the raw disagreement rate would be $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = 25\%$ for a perfectly secure channel. Sifting removes exactly this noise floor, which is what makes the QBER a meaningful security signal. Verify both numbers in the simulator.

Part 4: The Intercept-Resend Attack — QBER = 25%

Eve's simplest attack: intercept each qubit, measure it in a random basis $\gamma_i \in \{Z, X\}$, and resend the post-measurement state to Bob. She cannot do better than guess — the basis announcements happen only after Bob has measured. Condition on a sifted round ($\beta = \alpha$) and split on Eve's guess:

Case 1 — Eve guesses right ($\gamma = \alpha$, probability $\tfrac12$). Matched-basis measurement is deterministic: Eve reads $e = a$, resends the very same state, Bob recovers $b = a$.
Error probability: $0$
Case 2 — Eve guesses wrong ($\gamma \neq \alpha$, probability $\tfrac12$). Her outcome is a coin flip, and her resent state is a $\gamma$-basis eigenstate. Bob measures it in $\alpha \neq \gamma$ — mutually unbiased again — so his outcome is uniform regardless of what Alice sent.
Error probability: $\tfrac12$
$$Q \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac12 \cdot 0}_{\gamma = \alpha} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12}_{\gamma \neq \alpha} \;=\; \tfrac14 \;=\; 25\% .$$

Two refinements you can test directly in the simulator:

  • Partial interception. If Eve intercepts each round independently with probability $p$ and lets the rest pass, errors arise only on intercepted rounds: $Q(p) = p/4$, linear in $p$. Less interception means less information for Eve and less visibility — but never zero of one with the other.
  • Fixed-basis Eve. If Eve always measures in $Z$ instead of guessing randomly, the arithmetic changes but the answer does not: her matching rounds contribute $0$, the others $\tfrac12$ — still $Q = \tfrac14$. Randomizing her basis neither helps nor hurts her visibility.
Where Procedure 1 vs Procedure 2 becomes operational. "Resend the post-measurement state" hides a subtlety. If Eve measures by Procedure 1, her post-measurement state is the $\gamma$-basis eigenstate $|e_\gamma\rangle$ — resending it gives the 25% above. If she measures by Procedure 2 (apply $U_\gamma$, Z-measure), her post-measurement state is the computational state $|e\rangle$, and to run the same attack she must re-encode it by applying $U_\gamma^\dagger$ before resending. Done correctly, the two implementations are indistinguishable — same states to Bob, same $Q = 25\%$.

But suppose she forgets the re-encoding and naively resends $|e\rangle$ as-is. Work it through with the Born table:
  • Alice's $Z$-rounds: Eve's $\gamma = Z$ causes no error; $\gamma = X$ scrambles $e$ to a coin flip that Bob then reads deterministically → error $\tfrac12$. Contribution: $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$.
  • Alice's $X$-rounds: Bob X-measures a Z-eigenstate — coin flip whatever Eve's basis was → error $\tfrac12$. Contribution: $\tfrac12$.
$$Q_{\text{naive}} = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac14 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac38 = 37.5\% \;\neq\; 25\% .$$ Every outcome probability in her measurement was identical between the two procedures — yet the attack statistics changed, because the post-measurement states differ and the resent state is a post-measurement state. This is the Lab 5 distinction made operational. The simulator's "naive resend" mode lets you watch the QBER converge to 37.5% instead of 25%.

Part 5: Catching Eve — Detection Probability

Under full interception, each of the $k$ publicly compared check bits is wrong independently with probability $\tfrac14$. Eve escapes only if all $k$ agree:

$$P(\text{undetected}) = \left(\tfrac34\right)^k \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P_{\text{det}}(k) = 1 - \left(\tfrac34\right)^k .$$
check bits $k$151020
$P_{\text{det}}$ 25%76.3%94.4%99.7%

Detection is exponentially certain in the number of check bits. For partial interception the same argument gives $P_{\text{det}}(k; p) = 1 - (1 - p/4)^k$. Note the $k = 0$ limit: no check bits, no detection — the public comparison is not an optional optimization, it is the security test.

Honest security context. What you derived here covers one specific attack. The full security statement for BB84 (Shor & Preskill, 2000) says the protocol with error correction and privacy amplification produces a secure key whenever the QBER stays below ≈ 11% — against any attack physics allows, not just intercept-resend. Intercept-resend's 25% sits far above that threshold, which is why this attack is always catastrophic for Eve. The proof machinery is beyond this lab; the QBER-threshold logic — measure the error rate, abort above threshold — is exactly what you practice here.

Exercise 1 — Sifting statistics Mode 2

With Eve off, run N = 100, then N = 1000, then N = 5000 (same seed). Record the sifted fraction each time.

  • How does the deviation from $1/2$ shrink as N grows? Compare with the statistical scale $\sqrt{1/(4N)}$.
  • Confirm the QBER is exactly 0 in every run — not just small. Why is this exact even though the simulation is random?
Exercise 2 — Measuring Eve Mode 1 + 2

Turn Eve on (intercept-resend, p = 1).

  • In Step-by-Step mode, find a round where Eve guessed the wrong basis but no error resulted. Why is that possible? (Hint: Case 2 gives an error with probability $\tfrac12$, not $1$.)
  • In Batch mode with N = 5000, verify the QBER lands near 25%. How large is the deviation? Change the seed a few times — does it stay within a band of roughly $\pm 2\sqrt{0.25 \cdot 0.75 / (N/2)}$?
Exercise 3 — The naive resend: post-states matter Procedure 1 vs 2

Switch the Eve strategy to naive resend (she measures via Procedure 2 and forgets to re-encode with $U_\gamma^\dagger$). Run N = 5000.

  • Verify the QBER converges near 37.5%, not 25%.
  • Every measurement probability in Eve's apparatus is identical in the two strategies. What, physically, is different about what Bob receives?
  • Alice's Z-rounds and X-rounds contribute differently ($\tfrac14$ vs $\tfrac12$). Which rounds in the Step-by-Step log show it?
Exercise 4 — Detection budget Detection panel

Suppose Alice and Bob want to catch a full intercept-resend Eve with at least 99% probability.

  • From $P_{\text{det}}(k) = 1 - (3/4)^k$, compute the smallest sufficient $k$. Verify with the detection panel.
  • Repeat for a lazier Eve with $p = 0.25$. How many check bits do you need now? What does this say about the cost of detecting weak eavesdropping?
